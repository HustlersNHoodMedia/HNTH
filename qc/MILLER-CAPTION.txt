Doris "Dorie" Miller was a sharecropper's son from Waco, Texas. When he enlisted in 1939, mess attendant was one of the only jobs the segregated Navy allowed Black men to hold. He cooked, served food, collected laundry.

On December 7, 1941, he was collecting laundry when the bombs started falling on Pearl Harbor. He carried wounded sailors to safety. He tended to his dying commanding officer. Then he grabbed an anti-aircraft gun he had never been trained to use and fired at the planes until the ammunition ran out.

He became the first Black sailor ever awarded the Navy Cross. Admiral Nimitz pinned it on his chest personally, aboard the USS Enterprise.

In 2020, the Navy announced the first aircraft carrier ever named for a Black man: the USS Doris Miller. This week, reports revealed the Navy is discussing renaming the ship, and that internal documents already refer to it only by its hull number, CVN-81. His name quietly disappeared from the paperwork before anything was announced.

Black veterans are now publicly fighting to keep his name on it. The Navy is also recommending him for the Medal of Honor. Eighty-four years later, it still has not been approved.
