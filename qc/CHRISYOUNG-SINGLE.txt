He spent four years in a county jail waiting for his trial, and he used them to build a school out of nothing.

Chris Young grew up in Clarksville, Tennessee, in the kind of poverty where the only question is how to get out. Two small drug arrests as a teenager. Then at 22, a federal drug conspiracy case. Because of the two priors, the "three strikes" law made the sentence mandatory: life without parole, twice over.

Waiting for that trial, he read more than a thousand books. Economics, physics, history. He taught himself to program without ever touching a computer. He wrote his own curriculum and told himself the cell was a dorm room.

Then he learned about allocution, the right to speak to the court before sentencing. He spent months writing and memorizing a 45-minute statement. His reason: "I wanted to be seen. I wanted the judge to know I was a person."

Judge Kevin Sharp thought five years would have been fair. The law gave him no room. He handed down two life sentences, then walked away from a lifetime appointment to the federal bench in protest and started fighting to get Young out. Attorney Brittany K. Barnett worked the case for four years. Kim Kardashian took it to the White House. In January 2021, on the last night of that term, the clemency came through.

On May 16 of this year, Young graduated from SMU with a degree in economics and public policy. Sharp flew in from San Diego for it, with a handwritten card waiting in the car.

Young is 37 and has sickle cell anemia. He runs a consulting firm now. His memoir came out this month.

His line about the man he used to be: this Chris wouldn't exist without that Chris.
