He went in at 18 and came home at 30. Eleven years and six months, including a year in solitary.

When Kerwin Pittman walked out in January 2018 and went back to Southeast Raleigh, he had family, so he had somewhere to sleep. Most of the men he came out with didn't. He also had to relearn the basics nobody warns you about. How to drive. How to use a debit card.

So he started a nonprofit. Then mobile reentry centers built into RVs that drive across North Carolina. The first one served more than 3,000 people in 70 days.

Then he saw a listing. The Wayne Correctional Center in Goldsboro, 400 beds on 19 acres, shut since 2013 and sitting empty. He toured it. Dust everywhere, birds nesting inside, his own team looking at him like he had lost it. He says he stood in there and saw people walking around free.

He offered $275,000, money he raised from foundations and fellowships, and told himself he wouldn't go a dollar higher. The Council of State approved it in November. He is, as far as anyone can tell, the first formerly incarcerated person in the country to buy a prison.

It will take about $2 million to finish. The barbed wire comes down. The bars come out. The signs that say no inmates past this point come off the walls. Private rooms instead of open dorms, so it feels like a college dorm and not the thing it was. Up to 300 men at a time, six months each, learning HVAC, masonry, welding, plumbing.

And the state told him something he didn't know: the $275,000 he paid goes back into improving the prisons he used to live in.
